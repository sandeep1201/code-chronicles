# How to Approach Any System Design Problem

I had the fundamentals down — [caching](/blog/system-design-caching-strategies-and-pitfalls),
[load balancing](/blog/system-design-load-balancing-explained),
[CAP trade-offs](/blog/system-design-cap-theorem-consistency-availability). Deeper
posts on [consistent hashing](/blog/system-design-consistent-hashing),
[idempotency](/blog/system-design-idempotency-and-delivery-guarantees), and
[event sourcing](/blog/system-design-event-sourcing-and-cqrs) filled in the gaps. I could explain
each concept in isolation.

Then an interviewer said: **"Design Twitter."** Forty-five minutes on the clock.
I jumped straight to Kafka, Cassandra, and a CDN — and spent the last fifteen
minutes backtracking because I never asked who the users were, what "post a
tweet" actually meant for write volume, or whether we cared about read-your-writes
on the home timeline.

The concepts weren't the gap. **Process** was. A good system design answer —
in an interview or a real architecture review — is not a vocabulary dump. It's a
structured conversation: clarify what you're building, bound the problem, sketch
the shape, then go deep where the constraints bite.

This post is the framework I wish I'd had before that interview. The next posts in
this series cover [back-of-envelope math](/blog/system-design-back-of-envelope-math)
and [diagrams that expose trade-offs](/blog/system-design-diagrams-and-trade-offs).

**Intended audience**: developers who've worked through the fundamentals and several
advanced concept posts and want a repeatable process for open-ended design — whether
you're whiteboarding with a hiring manager or leading a design doc review for a feature
your team will actually ship.

**Prerequisites**:
- Fundamentals — at least [Scalability](/blog/system-design-scalability-scaling-up-vs-out), [Databases at Scale](/blog/system-design-databases-sql-vs-nosql-replication-sharding), and [Performance Metrics](/blog/system-design-performance-metrics-latency-throughput)
- Advanced topics (helpful, not mandatory): [Consistent Hashing](/blog/system-design-consistent-hashing), [CDN and Edge Caching](/blog/system-design-cdn-and-edge-caching), [Idempotency](/blog/system-design-idempotency-and-delivery-guarantees)
- You do **not** need every advanced post before reading this — the framework stands alone; you'll reference concepts as you need them

## Interview vs Real-World Design

Same skills, different constraints. Confusing the two is how you over-engineer in
production or under-clarify in an interview.

| Dimension | System design **interview** | **Real-world** design |
|-----------|----------------------------|------------------------|
| **Time** | 35–45 minutes | Days to weeks (RFC, review, iteration) |
| **Depth** | One happy path + 1–2 deep dives | Edge cases, migrations, ops runbooks |
| **Stakeholders** | One interviewer playing "product" | PM, security, legal, SRE, finance |
| **Success** | Clear thinking, trade-offs, communication | Ship safely, measurable SLOs, cost |
| **Scope** | You *must* say what's out of scope | You *negotiate* scope with the org |
| **Data** | Assumptions stated aloud | Metrics, traces, and incident history |

In an interview, the interviewer wants to see **how you think under ambiguity** —
not whether you've memorized Twitter's architecture. In production, they want to
know **what breaks at 3 a.m.** and what you'll defer to v2.

Both benefit from the same skeleton:

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  Clarify[Clarify requirements] --> Scope[Scope and out-of-scope]
  Scope --> Estimate[Estimate if needed]
  Estimate --> HLD[High-level design]
  HLD --> Deep[Deep dives and trade-offs]
`} />

The difference is how long you spend on each box and how many boxes you open.

**Interview bias:** breadth first — boxes, arrows, numbers on the napkin, then
pick the hottest component. **Production bias:** clarify with real traffic and
failure modes, write down non-goals, iterate the doc after review comments.

## Step 1: Clarify Requirements

Never draw a database before you know what the system **does**. Split requirements
into **functional** (features, user flows) and **non-functional** (how well it
must perform).

### Functional requirements

Ask product-style questions until you can list 3–5 core capabilities:

- **Who** are the users? (consumers, admins, internal services)
- **What** are the primary actions? (create, read, search, notify)
- **What** is the unit of data? (tweet, order, file, session)
- **What** integrations matter? (mobile, web, third-party API)

Example for "design a URL shortener":

| Must have | Nice to have (often v2) |
|-----------|-------------------------|
| Shorten a long URL → short link | Custom aliases |
| Redirect short → long | Analytics dashboard |
| Links expire (optional policy) | A/B test destinations |

Write these down. They become your API surface and data model later.

Example for "design a payment system" (running example in this series):

| Must have | Nice to have (often v2) |
|-----------|-------------------------|
| Authorize + capture card payments via PSP | Subscriptions / recurring billing |
| Transaction history + merchant balance | Multi-currency |
| Fraud/risk check before PSP | In-house ML fraud models |

Clarifying question that saves you: **Is authorization separate from settlement?**
(Yes for cards — drives your API shape and state machine. Full case study:
[Design a Payment System](/blog/system-design-payment-system) *(coming soon)*.)

### Non-functional requirements

These drive architecture. If you skip them, you'll pick the wrong database:

| Category | Example questions |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Scale** | DAU? Reads vs writes ratio? Peak QPS? |
| **Latency** | p99 for redirect? Acceptable lag for search index? |
| **Availability** | 99.9% vs 99.99%? Acceptable downtime window? |
| **Consistency** | Strong read-after-write, or eventual OK? |
| **Durability** | Can we lose a minute of data in a disaster? |
| **Security / compliance** | PII, encryption, audit, data residency |

Tie non-functionals to user-visible behavior:

- "Home timeline must feel instant" → read latency + caching strategy
- "Payments must never double-charge" → idempotency + strong consistency on writes
  ([idempotency post](/blog/system-design-idempotency-and-delivery-guarantees))
- "Global users" → multi-region and CAP choices
  ([CAP theorem](/blog/system-design-cap-theorem-consistency-availability))

**Pro tip:** In interviews, state assumptions explicitly: *"I'll assume 100M DAU,
10:1 read:write, and we need 99.9% availability unless you say otherwise."*
Interviewers often correct you — that's a feature, not a failure.

## Step 2: Scope Control and "Out of Scope"

Open-ended prompts are intentionally too big. **Scoping is a skill**, not a
cop-out. Naming what's *out* of scope proves you can prioritize and saves you
from designing twelve microservices in forty minutes.

### Techniques that work

1. **MVP slice** — "v1 is shorten + redirect only; analytics in v2."
2. **Single region first** — "Design for one region; I'll note multi-region
   extension points." ([Multi-region](/blog/system-design-multi-region-and-active-active)
   when relevant.)
3. **Cap entity size** — "Tweets are 280 characters; no video in scope."
4. **Cap user scale tier** — "Optimize for 1M users, design so 100M is a scaling
   path, not day-one complexity."
5. **Explicit non-goals** — a short list read aloud or written on the board:

```text
Out of scope for this session:
- Admin moderation UI
- ML-based feed ranking
- End-to-end encryption
- Billing and monetization
```

### Why this matters in production

Design docs without a **Non-goals** section grow forever. PM adds "just one more
API"; security adds a requirement that contradicts latency. A written out-of-scope
list is a contract for v1. You can still document "future considerations" in a
separate section — that's different from committing to build them now.

If the interviewer pushes back ("what about search?"), you can say: *"I'd add
Elasticsearch in v2 once we validate core redirect traffic; for this session I'll
mention the extension point on the diagram but not design indexing in depth."*
That's senior-level scope control.

## Step 3: Capacity Estimation (When It Matters)

Not every problem needs math in the first five minutes. You need estimates when
the answer **changes the architecture**:

- Storage size → single DB vs sharding
  ([databases at scale](/blog/system-design-databases-sql-vs-nosql-replication-sharding))
- Write QPS → sync vs async, queue depth, partition count
- Bandwidth → CDN vs origin, payload shape
  ([CDN post](/blog/system-design-cdn-and-edge-caching))
- Fan-out (followers × posts) → push vs pull timeline design

Rough order-of-magnitude is enough in an interview:

```text
100M DAU × 2 reads/day ≈ 200M reads/day ≈ ~2.3K avg read QPS
Peak ≈ 3–5× average → ~10K read QPS (round up)
```

If your estimate implies 10 TB/day of logs, say so — that steers you toward
object storage and batch pipelines, not a single Postgres row.

**This post stops at when and why to estimate.** The full walkthrough — storage,
QPS, bandwidth, fan-out, and common shortcuts — is in the next post:
[Back-of-the-Envelope Math That Actually Matters](/blog/system-design-back-of-envelope-math)
*(coming soon)*.

Rule of thumb: clarify and scope first, sketch the boxes second, **then** run
numbers on the path that looks hottest. Estimates validate the diagram; they
shouldn't replace it.

## Step 4: High-Level Design Before Deep Dives

After requirements and a rough sense of scale, draw the **happy path** in five to
seven components. Clients → entry point → core services → storage → async where
needed. No SQL schema yet. No shard count yet.

### What a good high-level diagram shows

- **Major components** and data flow direction
- **Sync vs async** boundaries (HTTP vs queue)
- **Read vs write** paths if they diverge early
- **External dependencies** (CDN, email, payment provider)

Example shape for a read-heavy service:

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  Client[Clients] --> LB[Load balancer]
  LB --> API[API service]
  API --> Cache[(Cache)]
  API --> DB[(Primary DB)]
  API --> Queue[Message queue]
  Queue --> Worker[Async workers]
  Worker --> DB
  CDN[CDN] --> Client
`} />

Label arrows with the operation: `POST /shorten`, `GET /{id} redirect`,
`publish event`. The interviewer should see **one complete user journey** before
you zoom in.

### Order of operations

1. **API / interface** — REST endpoints or events (what the client calls)
2. **Data model (conceptual)** — entities and relationships, not indexes
3. **Storage choice** — SQL vs NoSQL vs cache vs object store *with one sentence why*
4. **Async path** — what can be eventual (notifications, search index, analytics)

Resist the urge to deep-dive the cache algorithm before the box exists. Get agreement
on the skeleton: *"Does this shape match the requirements we listed?"* Course-correct
now; it's cheap.

Deep dives come next — pick **one or two** areas where requirements and estimates
create tension (timeline fan-out, hot keys, consistency across regions).

## Step 5: Identify Bottlenecks and Trade-offs

Every design is a bundle of trade-offs. Strong candidates **name the bottleneck,
propose a mitigation, and say what they're giving up.**

### Common bottleneck patterns

| Symptom | Likely bottleneck | Directions to explore |
|---------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Read-heavy, hot keys | Database or cache | Cache-aside, CDN, read replicas, [consistent hashing](/blog/system-design-consistent-hashing) |
| Write-heavy spikes | Single primary, lock contention | Sharding, async ingestion, partition by key |
| Fan-out (one write → many reads) | Push timeline | Hybrid push/pull, precompute for celebrities |
| Global latency | Single region | Multi-region, edge cache, geo-routing |
| Exactly-once illusion | Duplicate delivery | Idempotency keys, outbox, dedup store |
| Coordination | Split brain, double leader | Leader election, fencing tokens — and when *not* to build your own ([leader election](/blog/system-design-leader-election-and-distributed-locks)) |

For each deep dive, use a mini framework:

1. **Problem** — "Celebrity with 50M followers breaks push fan-out."
2. **Options** — push all, pull all, hybrid (push normal, pull for celebrities).
3. **Trade-off** — push = fast read, slow write; pull = slow read, simple write.
4. **Choice** — hybrid + cache hot timelines; document staleness for pull path.

Connect to concepts you've already learned instead of inventing new buzzwords.
Interviewers notice when you say *"This is cache-aside with TTL; stampede risk if
the key expires together"* versus *"We'll use Redis."*

### Failure and ops (brief but valuable)

Even in interviews, one minute on **what breaks** scores well:

- What happens if the cache is empty? (DB load — [caching pitfalls](/blog/system-design-caching-strategies-and-pitfalls))
- What happens if a partition occurs? (CAP — availability vs consistency)
- How do you deploy schema changes without downtime?

Production design docs spend more time here. Interviews get credit for awareness.

## A 45-Minute Interview Timeline

Use this as a template, not a straitjacket. Adjust if the interviewer steers early.

| Time | Phase | What to deliver |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| **0–5 min** | Clarify | Functional + non-functional list; state assumptions |
| **5–8 min** | Scope | MVP features; explicit out-of-scope (2–3 items) |
| **8–12 min** | Estimate (if needed) | Rough DAU, QPS, storage — one napkin calculation |
| **12–22 min** | High-level design | Box diagram, main APIs, data entities, one happy path |
| **22–38 min** | Deep dives | 1–2 bottlenecks with options and trade-offs |
| **38–43 min** | Failure / extend | Single region → multi-region; cache miss; idempotency |
| **43–45 min** | Recap | Requirements → design → trade-offs in three sentences |

Signals that impress:

- **Checking in** — "Does this scope work before I go deeper?"
- **Alternatives** — "We could use X; I'd pick Y because…"
- **Numbers tied to decisions** — "10K QPS is why I'd add a cache layer here."

Signals that hurt:

- Silent drawing for twenty minutes
- Technology salad with no data flow
- No non-functionals until the interviewer asks

If you're running long, **cut a deep dive**, not clarification. A clear scoped design
with one solid trade-off beats three half-explained subsystems.

## Common Mistakes

### Jumping to the database schema

Tables and indexes before user stories → wrong keys, wrong sharding strategy.
Fix: requirements first, conceptual model second, physical schema last.

### Buzzword bingo

"Kafka, Cassandra, Redis, Elasticsearch" with no arrows between them. Fix: every
component earns its place against a requirement or bottleneck.

### Ignoring read/write ratio

Designing write-optimized storage for a 1000:1 read system (or vice versa). Fix:
ask the ratio early; it drives caching and replication choices from the fundamentals posts.

### No explicit assumptions

Interviewers can't help if you don't state DAU, latency targets, or consistency needs.
Fix: propose reasonable defaults and invite correction.

### Designing v2 in v1

Analytics, ML ranking, multi-region active-active, and admin tooling in one pass.
Fix: out-of-scope list + "extension point" on the diagram.

### Single deep dive on the wrong thing

Forty minutes on JWT vs session cookies when the hard problem is timeline fan-out.
Fix: ask where risk lives; prioritize the constraint that breaks first at scale.

### Forgetting the human

No recap, no check-ins, no trade-off language. Fix: narrate your thinking; treat
it as a pairing session, not a solo exam.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Process beats vocabulary** — clarify requirements, scope the MVP, sketch the shape, then deep-dive where constraints collide.
2. **Split functional and non-functional requirements early** — features define APIs; latency, scale, and consistency define infrastructure.
3. **Say what's out of scope** — in interviews and design docs; it's prioritization, not weakness.
4. **Estimate when numbers change the architecture** — rough QPS and storage prevent wrong one-size-fits-all choices; details live in the math post.
5. **High-level design before details** — five to seven boxes, one happy path, sync/async boundaries visible.
6. **Trade-offs are the product** — name the bottleneck, compare options, pick one, state what you sacrifice.
7. **Interview vs production differs in time and depth, not in structure** — the same steps apply; production adds edge cases, ops, and iteration.
